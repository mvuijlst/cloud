# Evidence dossier — painted arms in medieval/early-modern armorials (c. 1250–1600) with their blazons

Feasibility input for a browser generator that draws fictional arms *in the manner of a specific roll* and emits a correct blazon.
Access date for every link: **26 October 2026**. Conventions: **VERIFIED** = I opened the resource (or its API/manifest) in this session and the fact is read from it; **UNVERIFIED** = taken from a search-result snippet, a secondary page, or inferred. Quotations are verbatim from the source named. Companion files: `links.json` (every link with its verification flag) and `downloads/MANIFEST.md` (16 sample images, 115 MB).

Scope note on method: the user's criteria are "identifiable primary artifacts and their catalogue records, preferably high-resolution scans showing multiple makers, periods, or witnesses … record provenance, dating, variation, and any access or cultural constraints … distinguish intentional construction rules from copying errors and material effects." Each section ends with a short verdict against those criteria.

### 1.2 Observations that matter for a generator

* **Three institutions give true IIIF with full-size downloads and public-domain marks: BSB (four armorials), KBR (Gelre), e-codices (Zürich).** With the KB's Commons spreads, that is five rolls in three regions (Lake Constance, Bavaria/Swabia, Guelders/Holland) and three generations (c. 1340, c. 1395–1405, 1466–1490) at 2,500–6,900 px per page, i.e. roughly 10–20 px per millimetre — enough to measure line weights, shield geometry and pigment colour.
* **French witnesses (Bellenville, Toison d'Or, Le Breton) are the weak point**: Gallica blocked automated access outright; the only re-hosted Toison d'Or is 1024 px wide. A human in a browser can download Gallica pages, but it cannot be scripted from here.
* **English rolls are effectively offline** (BL since the October 2023 attack; Bodleian down on the day; College of Arms never digitised). The English tradition therefore has to be worked from *texts* (Timms, Clemmensen, Aspilogia, DBA) rather than scans.
* **Rights statements disagree even within one institution** (BSB manifests say PDM 1.0; DDB records for the same objects say CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). Quote the manifest field and keep a copy.
* Layouts differ by roll and matter for "in the manner of": Zürich = two rows of crested shields on a 12-cm strip, names in capitals; Gelre = 5×3 rectangles ruled, shields pre-drawn at the bottom ("pages lined in 5x3 rectangles with shields predrawn at bottom", Clemmensen Survey); Bellenville "pages ruled 4x5"; Beyeren = 3×3 named shields; Grünenberg = 2×3 crested achievements; Scheibler = one full-page achievement; Toison d'Or = "L (5*5)" and "K (4*5)" shield grids plus full-page mounted knights (Clemmensen Survey, ETO).

**Verdict vs criteria:** meets "identifiable primary artifacts with catalogue records and multiple makers/periods/witnesses" well for the German–Swiss and Low-Countries groups; the French and English groups are catalogued but not reachable at resolution from a script.

**Which pairs a drawing with a consistent text?** For the *scanned* rolls above, Clemmensen's Grünenberg, Zürich and Bergshammar editions give a blazon, a tincture code and a page code for every painted item (his Grünenberg App. C/D concordances map BSB pages to items; I used them to find Toggenburg on BSB p.162 and Nellenburg/Rapperswil on p.164). For Gelre and Beyeren there is no free item-level edition: the Wikimedia Commons "Folio NNr de l'Armorial de Gelre" gallery pages (after Bunel/Gallo/Dupont) give French blazons per shield and were enough to identify f.83r; Beyeren pages carry their own name legends but no blazons. The Clemmensen Ordinary (.mdb) cross-references all of them by sigla (GEL, BEL, BEY?, BHM, ZUR, GRU…), so a drawing→blazon→family chain exists for most of the German/Swiss/Low-Countries material, at the cost of parsing his compact notation.

**Verdict:** the "catalogue record that pairs a drawing with a text" exists and is free for Grünenberg, Zürich, Bergshammar and the English rolls; it is commercial (Pastoureau–Popoff) for Gelre/Bellenville/Toison d'Or.

### 3.2 Modern statements of the rules (quotable)

* **Order of blazon** — Fox-Davies, *A Complete Guide to Heraldry* (1909), pp. 99–100 (VERIFIED from the archive.org text): "The commencement of any blazon is of necessity a description of the field … in a 'party' coloured field, that colour or tincture is mentioned first which occupies the more important part of the escutcheon. Thus, in a field 'per bend,' 'per chevron,' or 'per fess,' the upper portion of the field is first referred to; in a coat 'per pale,' the dexter side is the more important; and in a coat 'quarterly,' the tinctures of the 1st and 4th quarters are given precedence…" The chapter continues with field → principal ordinary/charge → secondary charges → charges on charges → marks of cadency (standard sequence; the later pages were not re-read here).
* **Rule of tincture** — Pastoureau, *Universalis* "Héraldique – 2. Les règles du blason" (VERIFIED): "La règle interdit de superposer ou de juxtaposer deux couleurs appartenant au même groupe." and "Cette règle fondamentale existe dès l'apparition des armoiries, c'est-à-dire dès le début du XIIe siècle." Fox-Davies p. 86 (VERIFIED): "colour cannot be placed upon colour, nor metal upon metal. Now this is a definite rule which must practically always be rigidly observed." He then discusses the Jerusalem exception (argent/or) and, a page or two later in the same chapter (page number not read; text lines 4476–4488 of the archive.org transcription), that "this rule, which forbids colour upon colour, and metal upon metal, only holds with regard to supporters and crests when…" (exceptions for crests/supporters and for charges *over* a party field). Documented exceptions in the period rolls themselves: furs and "proper" are neutral; a label or bordure of a colour on a colour is tolerated as a brisure (Fox-Davies p. 86 continues with such cases).
* **Furs** — Fox-Davies pp. 85–87: vair and its variants; "To all intents and purposes British heraldry now or hitherto has only known vair and potent."
* **"Proper"** — Fox-Davies pp. 87–88: "charge which is termed proper. German heralds describe furs and…"; warns that "care should be taken that the charges are blazoned proper" when natural colour is meant.
* **Marks of cadency / differencing** — Fox-Davies pp. 477–485 ("MARKS OF CADENCY"); period practice is visible in the samples: Brederode in Gelre f.83r carries a **label** on the Holland lion (brisure of a cadet line of Holland), and the Beyeren witness quarters it instead.
* **Partition lines, ordinaries, charge grammar** — Fox-Davies chapters "The Field of a Shield" (pp. 85 ff.) and following; DrawShield's parser grammar (GPL) is a working formalisation (see §5).
* **Assumption of arms** — Bartolo §4 (above): anyone may assume arms not another's; the rolls show the consequence (identical simple arms for unrelated houses: Habsburg and Holland both *Or a lion gules*, files 02 and 10 in `downloads/`).

### 3.3 Frequency statistics (the "parameter distributions")

VERIFIED, with page references, from Michel Pastoureau, "L'origine des armoiries de Catalogne" (IEC repository PDF `00000453/00000077.pdf`; printed page numbers 58–60; volume/year not identified in the PDF):

* Corpus: "le dépouillement statistique d'environ **125000 armoiries**, nobles et roturières, des XIIe, XIIIe, XIVe et XVe siècles connues par les sceaux et par les armoriaux" (p. 58). Method: "indices de fréquence concernant chacune des figures et des couleurs utilisées par le blason médiéval, méthode qui se fonde sur le dépouillement statistique des armoriaux et des sceaux de la majeure partie de l'Europe occidentale" (p. 58).
* Charges: "Au Moyen Age, moins de 1 % des armoiries en sont chargées [pals/palés]; pour l'ensemble de l'Europe leur indice de fréquence est de **6 pour 1000**, alors qu'il est, par exemple, de **155 pour 1000 pour le lion** et de **82 pour 1000 pour la fasce et les fascés**" (p. 58, footnote 12: "M. PASTOUREAU, *Traité d'héraldique*, p. 123 et 136"). Regional: pals/palés "plus de 4 %" in Suisse romande and Montbéliard; 2–4 % in Franche-Comté, Basel diocese, Provence, Catalonia, Foix; 1–2 % in Savoie-Dauphiné-Nice and Aragon-Navarre-Béarn (p. 58).
* Tincture combination: "La combinaison or/gueules est la plus utilisée dans les armoiries du XIIe siècle; son indice de fréquence moyen est de **31 %** pour l'ensemble de l'Europe, mais il dépasse **40 %**" in the Burgundian–Provençal zone (p. 59); and the or/azur combination, "rare dans les armoiries de XIIe et XIIIe siècles", rises in France in the 14th c. "par imitation des armoiries du roi de France" (p. 59).

UNVERIFIED (secondary paraphrases of Pastoureau, no page seen): gules present in "60 %" of European arms in the 12th–13th c. (fr.wikipedia/wikimonde "Gueules"); ranking gules > argent > or > sable > azure with azure overtaking sable over time (en.wikipedia "Tincture"); azure in "une armoirie sur vingt" c. 1200 and "près d'une sur trois" c. 1400 (Universalis summary of *Bleu*). The *Traité d'héraldique* (1979; 3rd ed. 1997) pp. 123 and 136 are the primary tables; *Figures et couleurs* (1986) and *L'hermine et le sinople* (1982) were not accessible online.

What this means for the generator: the only *period* parameter table one can cite today is Pastoureau's, and it is a Europe-wide average over 125,000 arms from seals *and* armorials. Per-roll distributions (e.g. the lion share in Gelre vs Zürich) do not exist in print and would have to be counted from Clemmensen's coded blazons — which is feasible, because his `{OS, AGAB}` tincture strings and charge terms are regular.

**Verdict:** the construction rules are the best-documented part of this system (period treatises + modern codification + one quantitative corpus); what is missing is per-witness statistics, which the editions make countable.

### 4.1 Same arms, different witnesses (seen in the downloads)

Clemmensen's Survey records the mechanisms behind such differences: Gelre "Several segments copied into BHM (BGH, ran, clem) and ETO (clem)"; Bellenville "GEL possibly used as a major source for BEL … but not for direct copying (clem)"; Toison d'Or "Extensively copied into BHM, LYN, APA, ARS, RUE, CLE and NLU"; and for the Grünenberg pair "comparison of the GStA and BSB manuscripts shows that copying could only be done from GStA to BSB. The artisan of the BSB, probably Rudolf Stahel, changed the layout of several pages so that the copy is staggered" (Grunenberg.pdf, "The manuscripts and facsimiles"). His *Editing Armorials* (2017) treats 45 armorials in six copying groups (Toison d'or, Urfé, Bellenville-Gelre, Rineck, Bodensee, Ashmole).

### 4.2 Copying errors flagged in the editions (counts from the downloaded PDFs)

* Grünenberg edition: the word "variant" occurs 145 times, "misread(ing)" 4, "copyist" 2, "wrong" 4; e.g. "the lobster a misreading of the nettleleaf" (Holstein), "cross being a misreading, possibly reinforced by the emblem of the Deutsche Order"; "Banner on dx, emended from the faded tanne tint"; "Colours faded into yellow-brownish hue".
* Bergshammar blazons: "variant" 73 times; Zürich: 10. Clemmensen marks unidentified or doubtful items with "*" and "s.n." and keeps a separate "GRU Not Identified" list (365 items) and corrections file (117 items) — a ready-made error model.

### 4.3 Material effects recorded by the editor (Survey, armorials20.pdf)

* "Or is often faded into Ar" (Donaueschingen); "Or faded into Ar, several Or/Ar differences from other blasons of a family coat" and "Vt as tanne; some Or as Ar" (Stuttgarter Wappenbuch); "Or often faded to Ar, and Ar sometimes tinted to Or" (Virgil Raber); "some azure and sable faded into grey, e.g. p.62" (St Gallen-Haggenberg); "some colours very faded and difficult to discern, e.g. Or/Gu, Sa brownish" (Richental); "'or' very faded on field and major ordinaries" (Clémery). Grünenberg: "completely faded in some cases, but retouched in pencil as are parts of the legends".
* Silver-leaf darkening (argent reading as sable) is a commonplace of the handbooks (en.wikipedia "Argent": silver leaf "darkened", making argent "hard to tell apart from sable") — UNVERIFIED as a conservation finding for any specific roll above; the Nellenburg case is the kind of spot where it should be measured.
* Drawing conventions distinct from error: Gelre "crest of dragon's head as griffin's/eagle's heads"; Bellenville "probably several drawers and several colourists at work; rows filled from below in certain instances"; "helmet 3/4 or 1/1 profile; several types of helmet"; Toison d'Or: "two layouts … L (5*5) … K (4*5)", "painter-A for the tables … painter-B for the mounted figures" (all Clemmensen Survey). Zürich: Clemmensen argues the roll was "designed" by rows and strips and that Merz & Hegi's numbering "is artificial, and though comfortable, totally misleading" — order of reading is itself a construction rule.

### 4.4 Shield shape by period (evidence in hand)

Fox-Davies pp. 62–63 (VERIFIED): "the form known as the heater-shaped shield is to all intents and purposes the earliest shape which was used for armorial purposes" (with Geoffrey Plantagenet's enamel, d. 1150). In the samples: Zürich c. 1340 — flat-topped triangular heaters with straight sides; Gelre c. 1395 and Beyeren 1405 — heaters with slightly convex sides and a pointed base, crested helms frontal/profile; Grünenberg 1483 — round-bottomed "U" shields with mantling filling the field; Scheibler c. 1450–80 — full-page achievement, shield small relative to crest; Toison d'Or 1434 — rounded heaters in 5×5 grids and large equestrian figures. Pastoureau's *Traité* chapter on shield shapes and Neubecker's *Heraldry: Sources, Symbols and Meaning* were not opened (UNVERIFIED).

**Verdict:** this is the strongest fit with the user's "distinguish intentional construction rules from copying errors and material effects": the editions name the copying chains, flag misreadings explicitly, and record fading by tincture; the scans are good enough to measure what the editor only describes.

## 6. Access, legal and cultural constraints

**Scans.** BSB (PDM 1.0 in manifests; DDB prints CC BY-NC-SA for the same objects — keep the manifest), KBR (CC0), e-codices (CC0 for AG 2760), KB (general terms; "Vrij te gebruiken"; Commons PD) are all fine for a public generator that embeds measured parameters or even crops. Gallica: standard conditions (non-commercial free, commercial by licence — UNVERIFIED this session; page 403) and automated access blocked. KHM Ingeram: private use only. BL: images unavailable; text CC-BY. WappenWiki art: NC — do not reuse in a generator you might ever license commercially.

**Legal status of arms.** Scotland: Lyon King of Arms Act 1672 (legislation.gov.uk) makes the Lyon Register the authority and unregistered arms penalisable; prosecutions via the Procurator Fiscal (secondary sources; UNVERIFIED wording). England: College of Arms; the Court of Chivalry sat in *Manchester Corporation v Manchester Palace of Varieties* [1955] P 133 — use of a corporation's arms on a seal actionable, decorative display not (ICLR/wiki snippets). Canada: Trademarks Act s. 9(1)(n.1) — "armorial bearings granted, recorded or approved" by the Governor General become prohibited marks once public notice is given (statute text in search excerpts). Germany: no statute; family arms protected by analogy to the name right § 12 BGB (BPatG 26 W (pat) 27/18, 2023; Austrian OGH 1994 applying § 43 ABGB by analogy to a German family's arms) — UNVERIFIED beyond snippets. Spain: no current national regulation; Consejo de Estado 1995 limited the Castilla y León Cronista to civic heraldry and the Ministry of Justice said a Cronista's certificate has no official value (forum/Dialnet snippets, low reliability). Practical consequence: a *fictional* arms generator is lawful everywhere; what must be avoided is presenting generated arms as a real family's or a civic/state emblem.

**Collision with real arms.** Simple arms are shared by unrelated houses (Bartolo's rule of free assumption; *Or a lion gules* = Habsburg, Holland, many others — visible in files 02/10/14). A generator drawing from period distributions **will** reproduce real coats often (lion 15.5 % of all arms; or/gules 31 % of 12th-c. combinations). Checking: parse the emitted blazon and look it up in Clemmensen's Ordinary (medieval), Papworth 1874 (British), Rietstap 1884 (Europe), DBA (British medieval). None of these is a web API; the .mdb and the PD scans can be indexed locally. Expect that *every* one-charge coat collides; report collisions rather than suppress them (that is itself a historically honest output: "also borne by…").

**Cultural sensitivities.** ADL hate-symbol entries (search snippets): the Sonnenrad/"black sun" (a 1930s Nazi design — not a period charge), the "Celtic cross" (an ancient sun-cross; "primarily used by non-extremists" but the white-supremacist form is common), the **wolfsangel** (genuinely heraldic, "in the coats of arms of various towns", appropriated by SS units — punishable in Germany in organisational contexts), the **odal rune**. Period rolls also contain charges a modern audience reads badly: Moors' heads (Gelre f.62r Sardinia/Aragon), Saracen/Turkish figures in "imaginary" realm arms (Grünenberg's Ternionen/Quaternionen), the swastika-like fylfot in a few English and German coats (UNVERIFIED as to specific rolls). A generator working from measured distributions should either exclude these charge classes or label them as period material with context.

## Overall verdict against the user's criteria

* Identifiable primary artifacts with catalogue records, several makers/periods/witnesses, high-resolution scans: **yes**, for a Swiss–German–Low Countries core (Zürich, Gelre, Beyeren, Grünenberg, Scheibler, Wernigerode, Ortenburg) with CC0/PDM rights and IIIF; **no, from a script**, for France (Gallica blocked) and England (BL offline).
* Provenance, dating, variation recorded: **yes**, unusually well — Clemmensen's Survey and editions give dates with arguments, copying chains, and item-level "variant/misreading" flags.
* Construction rules vs copying errors vs material effects: **separable**, because the tradition wrote its own grammar (treatises, Fox-Davies, Pastoureau) and the editor annotates fading and misreadings; the one thing not yet done anywhere is to *measure* the per-roll distributions and drawing conventions from the pixels — which is exactly the work the user's sigil project did.
* Risk: the output of a faithful generator will collide with real arms constantly; that has to be a feature (lookup + "also borne by") not a bug.
